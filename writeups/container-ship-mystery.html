The Container Ship Mystery: Tracing the EVER GOLDEN

Finding the port where a container-ship photo was taken, using only a written description, by filtering candidates and cross-validating each clue.

GEO-INT IMINT Maritime OSINT Vessel Tracking

In this OSINT challenge we get a written description of a photo of a very large container ship, without the image itself. The task is simple but tricky: find the port where the photo was taken. The description contains many overlapping visual details. Taken one at a time, each works as a filter, and together they point to a single place.

01 Break down the maritime data

In maritime OSINT we first understand the main subject: the ship. The vessel is EVER GOLDEN (IMO 9811000), and it sails on the AEM7 service (Asia-Mediterranean).

We start here because a liner service follows a fixed rotation of ports. A ship can only be photographed in a port it actually calls at, so the rotation gives us a candidate list instead of the whole world. The ship's size (the source gives 20,000+ TEU) narrows it further, since only terminals that can handle this class qualify.

02 Filter by geography and topography

This is where IMINT observation starts. The description states:

On the right side of the frame, a historic fortress/castle with crenellated walls sits on a rocky promontory overlooking the harbor entrance.

This is our strongest filter. Many ports have an old fortification somewhere, so the fortress alone is not enough. What is rare is a modern, high-capacity container port whose harbor entrance is overlooked by a rocky promontory with a fortress on top.

So we go through the candidates one by one in Google Earth and compare the terrain around each harbor entrance (flat or elevated, which side the high ground is on, whether a fortification is there) with what the description says. We rule candidates out by looking at the imagery, not by assumption.

03 Cross-validate with infrastructure and colour

A good OSINT analyst never relies on a single clue, so we cross-validate with smaller details. The description includes three colour-coded ones:

Port cranes: Super Post-Panamax cranes painted entirely bright blue, instead of the red or white seen in most ports.

Navigation tower: a pilot tower at the end of the left breakwater, painted with horizontal red and white stripes.

Pilot boats: the pilot authority's boats have bright yellow hulls.

Any one of these could match many ports. The combination of all three is far more selective, which is why we use them to confirm or reject a candidate that already passed step 02.

04 Temporal analysis: light and season

The description gives a time of 07:45 local time and snow-covered mountains in the background. Snow on mountains points to the colder months, and low early-morning light is consistent with that. Clear winter mornings also allow long visibility.

We use this as a consistency check on a candidate, not as a filter on its own. It cannot separate two Mediterranean ports, but it can tell us whether a candidate's surroundings are plausible for the season implied by the description.

05 Limits of the evidence

We never saw the photo. Everything below comes from the challenge text and was not independently verified: the vessel name and IMO number, the AEM7 service and its port rotation, the ship's 20,000+ TEU size, the fortress description, the crane, tower and pilot-boat colours, the 07:45 local time and the snow-covered mountains. Our reasoning is only as reliable as that description.

KEY TAKEAWAY

Treat every detail as a filter on a candidate list, then cross-validate with independent clues. No single detail should decide the answer.
